> Backdated to November 18, when I first wrote these thoughts down.

A somewhat heavy thought has been crossing my mind lately: with AI moving this fast—to the point where people are seriously talking about AGI—finding a job as an undergraduate computer science student is probably going to be harder than it used to be.

That worry doesn't come out of nowhere. Whenever I write code, tinker with my site, or look things up, I can see firsthand how quickly AI is improving. Things that used to take hours of digging through documentation and trial and error now come back as a working version as soon as I explain what I want. A lot of routine coding, debugging, and organizing can already be handed straight to AI.

Purely as a user, stronger tools save a ton of effort. Still, the moment I think about entering the job market with a bachelor's degree in CS, I feel uneasy. It used to feel like learning a few programming languages and being able to build common features was enough to land an entry-level role; now the junior tasks that used to be where newcomers learned the ropes are gradually being taken over by models. By the time I graduate, I honestly don't know what those roles will look like, or how many people with just undergraduate fundamentals companies will still need.

I run into plenty of debates online about programmers and AI—some say you just need to use the tools well, others say entry-level jobs are shrinking fast. Both sides make sense in their own way, yet when it's your own future on the line, it's hard to settle on a tidy conclusion. I don't have a confident answer right now, and I can't pretend these shifts won't affect me.

For now, all I can really do is keep learning the fundamentals I need to learn, build small projects of my own with AI along the way, and see how things unfold. I'm writing this uncertainty down as it stands today, to look back on in a year or two.
